Case 1

Bringing Claude into a regulated firm

An agent platform was already in place and nobody had a Claude account. I made the case, rolled Claude out, and now ship Claude Code plugins and Cowork skills that colleagues run in Cowork without ever opening a terminal.

The operations teams are non-technical and their documents carry confidential investor data. I had to justify a second AI vendor to the Chairman and the leadership team, then get the tools I build into the hands of finance controllers and operations staff.

What I did

  • Built a total-cost-of-ownership model that costed Claude Team and Enterprise next to the incumbent platform, and a one-page note recommending a phased rollout. It won over the Chairman and leadership.
  • Started the rollout in May 2026 at the asset management company, then extended it to the group: 10 Team seats plus individual accounts. I own licences and access rights.
  • Trained 25 people through workshops, one-to-one sessions and written guides.
  • Set up a private organisation plugin marketplace and wrote the company playbook for sharing a Claude plugin.
  • Published the reporting plugin to the organisation, so its skills (a four-eyes review of investor reports, a fast review and a restyle to the house template) open in Cowork as well as Claude Code, with no terminal involved. It is a pilot with the finance controllers.

How Claude Code helped

  • Read the incumbent platform's usage export, researched public pricing and built the TCO workbook (12 tabs, 324 formulas) over five versions.
  • A research workflow established how the desktop app runs a skill locally for a Team user. A six-agent adversarial read of my setup guide then found two Windows blockers before it shipped.
  • Since v0.5.0, every release of the reporting plugin goes through publishability tests, a pull request, a version bump and a tag.

How I build

Write it down, test first, let agents attack the result, keep a human on the decision

In a regulated firm a wrong figure sent to investors is hard to take back, and a fluent answer proves nothing. I work across more than 90 workstreams, mostly alone, so my way of working has to make Claude's output checkable, resumable and safe to hand to non-technical colleagues.

Five rules I work by

  • Every session leaves a trail. One folder per workstream, a dated journal per session, a CLAUDE.md for rules that outlive the session.
  • A green test suite is a claim until an adversarial review has tried to break it. A five-agent review once caught a query that passed with one fund and returned nothing with two.
  • Keep the second pass blind. Pass B works in a fresh context and rewrites its logic without seeing pass A.
  • Make failure the safe default. When a match is doubtful, the tool falls towards NO-GO.
  • No source, no value. A fact without a located reference gets confidence 1 out of 5. Numbers are computed in deterministic code.

The tooling

  • The open-source superpowers skills for larger builds: brainstorming, written plans, subagent-driven and test-driven development.
  • The Workflow tool for fan-out work, with schema-bound outputs and resumeFromRunId when a limit cuts a run short.
  • /code-review at xhigh effort before a hand-over: detection angles in parallel, then a verifier on every candidate finding.
  • MCP to build and to verify: the app database, Notion, Google Drive, Gmail and Slack.
What I learned

Independence is a discipline. Twice during the manual campaign, before the plugin existed, the second pass shared a session it should not have; the journals record both.

Sub-agent reports are claims until I run their code. On the reporting plugin, running the chain exposed four defects the agents had not reported.

Case 2

The period-end workflow app

I designed and built it alone with Claude Code. In four weeks the firm's period-end tracking went from a spreadsheet to a secured production app the whole team works in: it ran the June 2026 period-end and logged 1,542 step updates by mid-September.

At each reporting date (30 June, 31 December), every fund's NAV (net asset value) and a set of recurring deliverables must be produced: 45 steps in five phases, with external fund accountants and four-eyes reviews. When I arrived this lived in spreadsheets, with no management view and no link between dependent steps.

What I built

  • Three versions in four weeks: a normalised Google Sheets base, an Apps Script app that stayed in test, then a rebuild on Next.js and PostgreSQL, live on 18 June.
  • A pure TypeScript domain core: fund health (red, amber, green), hard and soft dependencies between steps, absence cover.
  • Security by default: row-level security on every table for reads, writes only through a tested server layer, Google SSO with MFA and an allowlist, and an append-only audit log written in the same transaction as each update.
  • Role-based views for management, controllers and the transfer-agency team that keeps the investor register.

How Claude Code helped

  • Brainstorm, spec, plan, then one implementer sub-agent per task with a spec reviewer and a quality reviewer behind it. TDD on the core.
  • Adversarial reviews at every milestone; on the new backend, a five-agent review caught the bug shown below.
  • The Supabase MCP server in session to apply migrations and confirm row-level security, with my go-ahead on every production write.
  • Systematic debugging on exported platform logs when production broke.

What I learned

Case 3

Agentic quality control before anything reaches an investor

Two isolated passes read every document, a third arbitrates, and a confirmed error that only one pass caught still blocks the send.

A capital account statement shows one investor their commitment, paid-in capital, distributions and current value. Before it goes out, every figure must agree with the investor database, the NAV calculation, the auditor's certificate and the unit register. The goal: every figure on every statement checked, with a record of each check. The two failures that matter most are a wrong number and an investor who receives someone else's statement.

What I built

  • A review skill with three isolated passes: ten Python comparator modules (A), a vision sub-agent that writes its own checking code (B), and an arbiter (C).
  • Guardrails in the skill itself: no sampling, coverage proven one-to-one, isolation checked before C starts, STOP conditions such as a statement naming a different investor from its file.
  • Packaged as a Claude Code plugin with 300 tests and a setup guide for a Windows laptop.
  • The same pattern for investor reports: 137 controls and prescriptive fix sheets (page, what it says, what it should say, why).

How Claude Code helped

  • Five agents attacked the spec before any code: 50 findings, 11 blocking. One fix made the miss rule symmetric.
  • A /code-review confirmed four real bugs, one of which could have issued a false GO.
  • Evals ship as code: 10 fictitious funds with one trap each test the decision rule against pass A and a scripted stand-in for pass B, including a planted false negative that must end in NO-GO. The model's own reading is checked on a real fund instead: 189/189 values agreed within one cent.
  • Field feedback loop: two fixes shipped the day the first colleague reported problems, a parallel version the next day.

GO if and only if every check has zero blocking issue after arbitration by C, and every A/B divergence is resolved. Anything detected by only one of the two passes and confirmed on re-check is a false negative of the other pass. It is blocking, whichever pass saw it.

What I learned

A and B run on the same base model, so they can share a blind spot. They differ in how they read: parsing code for A, vision for B. My eval shows the residual risk: if B also misses the planted error, the verdict is GO.

The report engine read text well and tables badly, and failed silently. My fix in progress: have the model extract the data twice, independently, into a fixed schema, and keep every comparison in code.

Case 4

Making 45 fund agreements agent-ready

A Claude Code pipeline read every page of 45 fund agreements and turned them into facts an agent can count, search and quote.

Each fund has an agreement of 80 to 120 pages (an LPA, or fund rules for French funds) that sets fees, redemption rules and investor rights. There was no single record of what they say, and every question on a redemption or a transfer meant rereading a PDF.

What I built

  • One extraction agent per fund reading 20-page slices on nine dimensions, from tax-eligibility ratios to redemption terms, each fact with article, page, verbatim quote and confidence.
  • A sceptical checker per fund against the source. In the first run it corrected two funds.
  • A rule against invented citations: a reference the agent cannot find reads “reference not located”, confidence 1.
  • A hybrid knowledge agent with five playbooks, deployed on the firm's existing agent platform and now its most-used AI agent; Claude Code built the base it reads. A 724-line runbook lets a fresh session add a fund.

How Claude Code helped

  • Workflow scripts with one schema-typed agent per fund.
  • A five-agent research workflow settled the agent design first: counting goes to SQL, because semantic search ranks by relevance and misses items.
  • A per-fund completeness check found four agents that had stopped early; a four-agent run completed them.
  • I paused a 76-agent pass with 60 done and resumed it from cache, adding two funds received in between.

Case 5

Owning the numbers: KPIs in version-controlled SQL

Every KPI is defined once, as a BigQuery view under version control, and each team reads its own Looker Studio dashboard. I write the SQL with Claude Code.

Each reporting date triggers a set of recurring deliverables, and their deadlines lived in workbooks. I wanted one set of numbers that every team reads the same way: on-time delivery, anomalies with their resolution time and recurrence, and incidents, categorised, with remediation plans generated automatically.

One KPI view · synthetic: dataset, tables and columns are fictional

CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW `demo_ops.kpi_team_by_reporting_date` AS
WITH delivery AS (
  SELECT team, reporting_date,
    COUNT(*) AS items_due,
    -- an undelivered item compares as NULL, so it counts as late
    COUNTIF(delivered_on <= due_on) AS items_on_time
  FROM `demo_ops.deliverables`
  WHERE is_recurring AND is_applicable AND due_on <= CURRENT_DATE()
  GROUP BY team, reporting_date
),
resolution AS (
  SELECT team, reporting_date,
    COUNT(*) AS anomalies_logged,
    COUNTIF(resolved_on IS NULL) AS anomalies_open,
    -- approximate median days to resolve; open anomalies give NULL and are ignored
    APPROX_QUANTILES(DATE_DIFF(resolved_on, opened_on, DAY), 2)[SAFE_OFFSET(1)] AS median_days_to_resolve
  FROM `demo_ops.anomalies`
  GROUP BY team, reporting_date
)
SELECT team, reporting_date, items_due, items_on_time,
  ROUND(SAFE_DIVIDE(items_on_time, items_due), 3) AS on_time_rate,
  anomalies_logged, anomalies_open, median_days_to_resolve
FROM delivery LEFT JOIN resolution USING (team, reporting_date);

SQL in production

  • Under the workflow app: PostgreSQL with row-level security that fails closed and an audit log made append-only by triggers.
  • Production support in SQL, such as 30 backup assignments added for the August holidays with additive inserts, so no existing assignment changed.

How Claude Code helped

  • Writes and edits the KPI views and the app's row-level security policies from my definitions.
  • Works on the app database through the Supabase MCP server: migrations, diagnostic queries, advisors, and nothing written to production without my approval.
What I learned

About 70% on time still means three deliverables in ten were late. The dashboards measure the gain; the teams made it.

Field notes

Product feedback from the field: six things that broke or surprised me

I log what breaks and how I work around it. These come from dated session logs between May and September 2026, in one Team organisation. Where the fault was mine, I say so. I have not reproduced most of these in isolation, and behaviour may have changed since.

1. A manual upload silently shadowed the organisation's plugin

What happened
The organisation syncs a marketplace, so even one plugin needs one around it. Auto-sync fired when a merged pull request raised the version; a direct push waited for an admin to click Update. Meanwhile my own machine was still running a manual upload from 1 September, and three days of fixes never reached it.
Workaround
One private repository lists every plugin; each release of the reporting plugin goes through a pull request, a version bump and a tag; a release counts as delivered once I have seen it load on a member's machine.
Suggestion
A publisher view of which version each member runs and where it came from, and a warning when a manual upload shadows an organisation plugin.

2. A skill description over 1,024 characters dropped the skill silently

What happened
To stop two sibling skills competing for the same requests, I made their descriptions mutually exclusive. One grew to 1,112 characters, and the sync published the plugin without that skill. The only trace was a console warning, and 748 green tests missed it.
Workaround
Cut the description to 727 characters with every trigger kept; tests now check description length, frontmatter and the bundle hash, on a fresh clone before merge.
Suggestion
Fail the sync, or block the merge with a check, when a skill breaks a publication rule; and routing checks across all the skills an organisation publishes: trigger evals test one description at a time, while my collision was between two sibling skills.

3. The Gmail connector rewrites HTML drafts

What happened
Read back raw, my drafts had lost every image, links were rewritten to redirect URLs and the background property was stripped. An update returned a new message ID and a new thread, and the old IDs returned “not found”. Part of the rewriting may be Gmail's own sanitisation.
Workaround
The connector now writes the draft's structure; the final HTML goes to the clipboard for the user to paste, after a blocking pre-check on images, links and backgrounds.
Suggestion
Document the sanitisation rules, list what was stripped in the tool response, and say in the response which IDs changed.

4. The Notion connector under six parallel sub-agents

What happened
Splitting a 43-page update across six sub-agents produced fetches from the wrong page and confirmations for views that did not exist. Later, a write returned success but dropped the value.
Workaround
Sequential calls in batches of six or seven, a person checks one page before any bulk change, and a full audit afterwards.
Suggestion
A per-connector concurrency budget shared across sub-agents, with throttling returned as an explicit error. Part of this sits with Notion, and I would raise that part with them.

5. A sub-agent committed outside its worktree

What happened
During a plan run, one sub-agent committed to main in the primary checkout instead of the session's worktree: two stray commits. I have not pinned down why.
Workaround
I rewrote the two files on the session branch and reset main by hand. My plugin release guide, written later, forbids direct pushes to main.
Suggestion
Warn, or ask, before a sub-agent commits outside the session's worktree and branch.

6. Usage limits cut verification passes

What happened
The weekly limit stopped an eight-lens adversarial check after four lenses, before its refutation phase ran. In another workflow, the session limit cut off the last 12 agents of a 30-agent run.
Workaround
There, resumeFromRunId replayed the 18 finished agents from cache and re-ran only the 12 missing ones, which worked well. For the eight-lens check, I ran the four missing lenses directly in the session, without agents, and triaged the findings by hand. I now size workflows to the stakes.
Suggestion
Estimate a workflow's usage before launch, warn before it crosses the limit, and let me choose which phase to protect.

What I'd bring to founders and VCs

What carries over from this year

  1. I have sat in your seat. I co-founded and ran a B2B fintech that sold to private-equity funds: I owned the product, raised a pre-seed round and led a pivot from client feedback. I back three startups and have mentored 25+ student founding teams.
  2. VCs run funds too. Capital account statements, LP reporting and fund-agreement terms are the back office I have been rebuilding with Claude. I would start with a fund's platform team on its own operations, then take what works to its portfolio companies.
  3. I have been the regulated buyer. This year I made the case for a second AI vendor to a Chairman and costed it against the incumbent. Founders selling into finance need output a buyer can check: a blind second pass, an arbiter, no source, no value.
  4. Adoption starts with one task that hurts. Build the skill with the person who owns the task, a one-page guide in plain language, an eval with a planted failure, then a release playbook: one private marketplace, a pull request, a version bump and a tag. The skills and guides I write for my teams are in French.
  5. ROI on a business outcome. I made the case with a TCO model and report on on-time delivery and errors stopped before sending; agents and tokens I count as spend. Time saved per task, against a timed manual baseline, is my next measure.
